How Landturned estimates change

Impact Observatory labels the land. Landturned compares the years, counts the change, and lays it over satellite photos.

Impact Observatory is a mapping company. Every year, its computer model looks at that year's pictures from Sentinel-2, two European satellites that photograph all of Earth's land every few days, and gives every 10 m square of land one label, such as trees, crops or buildings. The result is a land-cover map of the whole world, published free for anyone to use.

Landturned reads those maps for every year from the first year to the latest year and counts the pixels that changed. The results are estimates of change from satellite-based maps, not a survey and not exact science. This page shows how they are made, and where they can go wrong.

01From address to answer

Six steps, every time. Diagram, not real data

  1. Pick a place

    Type an address, pick a spot on the globe, or use your location.

  2. Draw the square

    2 × 2 km around the spot: 400 hectares.

  3. Read the yearly maps

    Impact Observatory's map for every year, the first year to the latest year. Their 9 classes become our 5: T trees, G grass or farmland, B built, W water, S bare ground (soil, sand, rock, snow).

  4. Compare each pixel

    Same pixel, first year against a later one. If its label changed, it gets a change color. Changes narrower than about 30 m are ignored, to remove noise.

  5. Count

    Each pixel is 0.01 ha, so pixels × 0.01 = hectares of change.

  6. Lay it on the photo

    The change layer is drawn over Esri satellite photos taken close to those years. The photos are sharper than the 10 m measurement.

02The square, in pixels

Every answer covers the same square, and the maps behind it are made of pixels.

  1. The square

    2 × 2 km around the spot: 400 hectares. Every result describes exactly this square.

  2. Split into pixels

    200 pixels across, 200 down: 40,000 pixels. The real grid is too fine to draw here, so this shows one line every 10 pixels.

  3. One pixel

    10 × 10 m = 100 m² = 0.01 ha. Each pixel gets one label per year, so anything smaller, like a shed or a single tree, blends into it.

03Their 9 classes, our 5

Impact Observatory sorts land into 9 classes. Landturned merges them into 5 categories, plus “no data”.

  • Three green classes become one

    Crops, rangeland (grass and shrubs) and flooded vegetation (wetlands, rice) are all open, green land. The model can also mix them up from one year to the next. Merged, a field turning into pasture isn't counted as change.

  • Trees stay apart, but count as green

    Trees and grass or farmland are both green space. So forest cut for pasture gets no color: green stayed green. Land stripped bare does.

  • Snow and ice count as bare

    Open land with nothing growing on it, like rock and sand.

  • Clouds become “no data”

    Where clouds hid the ground in either year, nobody can tell what changed. Those pixels are never counted as change; each result reports them separately.

04Which color a change gets

Each 10 m square gets a color based on what the land was and what it became.

  • Newly built

    Field → warehouse. Anything that becomes built.

  • New water

    Valley → reservoir. Anything that becomes water.

  • Green space cleared

    Forest → bare dirt. Green land stripped bare, with nothing built yet, like a building site.

  • Green space gained

    Parking lot → park. Built, water or bare land that turns green.

No color: Forest → farmland, because both count as green space. Built or water that becomes bare ground, like a demolished building or a drained pond. And squares with no data (clouds), which each result reports separately.

Green space lost = everything green that isn't green anymore: the red pixels, plus orange and blue pixels that used to be trees, grass or farmland.

05Cleaning the edges

Changes narrower than about 30 m are removed, one color at a time. Diagram, not real data

  1. Raw change

    Along a shoreline, a field edge or a road, a 10 m pixel is partly one thing and partly another, so its label can flip between years although nothing changed. These flips show up as thin lines and specks.

  2. Shrink by one pixel

    Every colored patch loses its outer ring of pixels. Anything narrower than 3 pixels (30 m) disappears completely.

  3. Grow back by one pixel

    What's left grows back to its full shape. The thin lines and specks have nothing left to grow from, so they stay removed. Wide patches keep their shape, but thin spurs sticking out of them are trimmed too.

The catch: a real change narrower than 30 m, like a single new house or a narrow path, is removed too. In image processing and GIS this shrink-then-grow step is called a morphological opening.

06Why it's an estimate

Five things that can make the numbers too high or too low. Diagram, not real data

  • The label can be wrong

    A computer model made the maps, and it sometimes mislabels a pixel: a dry field can look like bare ground. A wrong label in either year looks like change. A whole mislabelled field is big enough to survive cleaning, so it shows up as change. Impact Observatory reports an average accuracy of over 75% for its yearly maps.

  • Edges are fuzzy

    A pixel on a shoreline or a road is partly one thing and partly another, so its label can flip. Cleaning (05) removes most of this, and real changes under 30 m with it.

  • Clouds hide the ground

    Where clouds covered a pixel in either year, nobody can tell what changed. It counts as no data, never as change, so real change under the cloud is missed.

  • 10 m is coarse

    One pixel is 10 × 10 m, about the size of a small house, and gets one label. A shed or a single tree blends into whatever surrounds it.

  • Photo and map dates differ

    The satellite photos are for looking only. They can be from a different year than the maps, so the colors may not line up with what you see.

So read the numbers as good estimates of change at the scale of a street or a neighborhood: reliable for a new suburb, a factory or a reservoir, not for a single building.

07Where you can look

Each search opens a 20 × 20 km box around the address. Pan the map (or drag the dashed square) and press Analyze this area to measure any 2 × 2 km square inside it. Squares near the edge stop a little short, so the result fits on screen. Keeping each search to one area keeps results fast, and keeps the free data sources we rely on available for everyone. To look further away, search a new address: the box moves with it.
