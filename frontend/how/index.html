How Landturn estimates change

Impact Observatory labels the land. Landturn compares the years, counts the change, and lays it over satellite photos.

Impact Observatory is a mapping company. Every year, its computer model looks at that year's pictures from Sentinel-2, two European satellites that photograph all of Earth's land every few days, and gives every 10 m square of land one label, such as trees, crops or buildings. The result is a land-cover map of the whole world, published free for anyone to use.

Landturn reads those maps for every year from the first year to the latest year and counts the pixels that changed. The results are estimates of change from satellite-based maps, not a survey and not exact science. This page shows how they are made, and where they can go wrong.

01From address to answer

Six steps, every time. Diagram, not real data

  1. Pick a place

    Type an address, pick a spot on the globe, or use your location.

  2. Draw the square

    2 × 2 km around the spot: 400 hectares.

  3. Read the yearly maps

    Impact Observatory's map for every year, the first year to the latest year. Their 9 classes become our 5: T trees, G grass or farmland, B built, W water, S bare ground (soil, sand, rock, snow).

  4. Compare each pixel

    Same pixel, first year against a later one. If its label changed, it gets a change colour. Changes narrower than about 30 m are ignored, to remove noise.

  5. Count

    Each pixel is 0.01 ha, so pixels × 0.01 = hectares of change.

  6. Lay it on the photo

    The change layer is drawn over Esri satellite photos taken close to those years. The photos are sharper than the 10 m measurement.

02The square, in pixels

Every answer covers the same square, and the maps behind it are made of pixels.

  1. The square

    2 × 2 km around the spot: 400 hectares. Every result describes exactly this square.

  2. Split into pixels

    200 pixels across, 200 down: 40,000 pixels. The real grid is too fine to draw here, so this shows one line every 10 pixels.

  3. One pixel

    10 × 10 m = 100 m² = 0.01 ha. Each pixel gets one label per year, so anything smaller, like a shed or a single tree, blends into it.

03Their 9 classes, our 5

Impact Observatory sorts land into 9 classes. Landturn merges them into 5 categories, plus “no data”.

  • Three green classes become one

    Crops, rangeland (grass and shrubs) and flooded vegetation (wetlands, rice) are all open, green land. The model can also mix them up from one year to the next. Merged, a field turning into pasture isn't counted as change.

  • Trees stay apart, but count as green

    Trees and grass or farmland are both green space. So forest cut for pasture gets no colour: green stayed green. Land stripped bare does.

  • Snow and ice count as bare

    Open land with nothing growing on it, like rock and sand.

  • Clouds become “no data”

    Where clouds hid the ground in either year, nobody can tell what changed. Those pixels are never counted as change; each result reports them separately.

04Which colour a change gets

Each 10 m square gets a colour based on what the land was and what it became.

  • Newly built

    Field → warehouse. Anything that becomes built.

  • New water

    Valley → reservoir. Anything that becomes water.

  • Green space cleared

    Forest → bare dirt. Green land stripped bare, with nothing built yet, like a building site.

  • Green space gained

    Parking lot → park. Built, water or bare land that turns green.

No colour: Forest → farmland, because both count as green space. Built or water that becomes bare ground, like a demolished building or a drained pond. And squares with no data (clouds), which each result reports separately.

Green space lost = everything green that isn't green any more: the red pixels, plus orange and blue pixels that used to be trees, grass or farmland.

05Cleaning the edges

Changes narrower than about 30 m are removed, one colour at a time. Diagram, not real data

  1. Raw change

    Along a shoreline, a field edge or a road, a 10 m pixel is partly one thing and partly another, so its label can flip between years although nothing changed. These flips show up as thin lines and specks.

  2. Shrink by one pixel

    Every coloured patch loses its outer ring of pixels. Anything narrower than 3 pixels (30 m) disappears completely.

  3. Grow back by one pixel

    What's left grows back to its full shape. The thin lines and specks have nothing left to grow from, so they stay removed. Wide patches keep their shape, but thin spurs sticking out of them are trimmed too.

The catch: a real change narrower than 30 m, like a single new house or a narrow path, is removed too. In image processing and GIS this shrink-then-grow step is called a morphological opening.

This is a real corner of Ontario Ranch, California: 400 × 400 m, or 1,600 pixels. On the left are the labels for 2017, on the right the labels for 2023. Farmland turned into houses, and one area was still a bare construction site.

Move the slider to compare 2017 with any later year, and switch the edge cleaning on or off. The hectares are recounted as you go, the same way Landturn counts them for a whole square.

Why it's an estimate

The labels can be wrong
Computers made these maps from satellite images, and they sometimes give a pixel the wrong label: a dry field can look like bare ground, a desert like grassland. A wrong label in either year looks like a change.
Edges are fuzzy
A pixel on a shoreline or along a road is partly one thing and partly another, so its label can flip from year to year although nothing changed. That is why Landturn leaves out changes narrower than 30 m (three pixels). It also means a real change narrower than 30 m, like a single new house, is not counted.
Clouds hide the ground
Where clouds covered a pixel in either year, nobody can tell what changed. Those pixels are counted separately as "no data", never as change.
Ten metres is coarse
One pixel is 10 × 10 m, roughly the footprint of a family house. Anything smaller, like a garden shed or a single tree, disappears into its surroundings.

So read the numbers as good estimates of change at the scale of a street or a neighbourhood: reliable for a new suburb, a factory or a reservoir, not for a single building.
